\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Computational Environment}

Everything ran on Google Colab with one NVIDIA L4 GPU (about 23 GB of memory). The software was Python 3.13, PyTorch 2.14.1, Transformers 5.22.1, sentence-transformers 5.7.0, faiss-cpu 1.16.0, rank\_bm25 0.2.2, SciPy 1.16.3, NumPy 2.4.6 and, for the demonstration page, Gradio 6.31.0. The code, the training configuration and the per-question predictions are saved, so every number below can be recomputed from them.

\subsection{Implementation Details}

Before the full run I trained for 150 steps twice, once in bfloat16 and once in 32-bit precision. The mean loss was 14.24 and 14.25, neither run overflowed, and bfloat16 used less memory (14.4 GiB against 17.9 GiB) at the same speed (about 28 examples per second), so the full run used bfloat16. The full run processed about 29 examples per second. One epoch took between 405 and 411 seconds of training plus 17 to 57 seconds of validation, so the ten epochs took about 76 minutes. Scoring all 1,493 test questions in one condition took under a minute (54 seconds for the gold condition). Searching the library for all test questions took 11 seconds with BM25, 49 seconds with dense retrieval and 44 seconds with the hybrid, after the passage embeddings had been computed once.

After the first epoch the model answered every validation question with an empty string. That scores 24.1\%, which is exactly the share of unanswerable questions in the validation set, and it shows that at this point the model had learned nothing except that empty is the most common target. From the second epoch it started to produce real answers. Validation F1 reached 76.0 at epoch 9 and dropped slightly to 75.7 at epoch 10 while the training loss kept falling. I kept the epoch 9 checkpoint (validation EM 59.1, F1 76.0). Between epochs 7 and 10 the F1 moves within 1.3 points, which is about the size of the sampling error on 1,484 questions, so the flat end of the curve should not be over-read.

Over all questions the model is 2.5 EM points and 2.0 F1 points below the published scores. That is inside the three-point margin I set in advance for a successful reproduction. With 1,493 questions the sampling error of an EM near 0.6 is about 1.3 points, so a gap of this size is about two standard errors.

The gap comes entirely from the unanswerable questions. On answerable questions the model is slightly better than the published numbers (56.05 against 54.89 EM, 77.61 against 75.73 F1). On unanswerable questions it is right 71.5\% of the time against 85.4\%, which means it writes an answer to about 28\% of the questions that have none. Weighting the three differences by the share of each group gives back the overall gap. The model made about 7,450 updates against roughly 89,000 for the published run, and its validation score on unanswerable questions had climbed from 53.9 at epoch 4 to 67.9 at epoch 9 (66.2 at epoch 10). That fits the idea that more training would close the gap, but I did not run a longer training to test it.

Table~\ref{tab:hitmiss} explains most of the differences between the retrievers. When the right passage is returned, the reader scores about 57\% EM and 79\% F1 with all three retrievers. When a wrong passage is returned, EM falls to 8--12\% and more than half of the answers are empty. Without any passage the model answers almost nothing (2.0\% EM on answerable questions), and 96.6\% of its answers are empty. So the reader holds almost no knowledge of its own, and what it knows comes from the passage.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The question behind the project was how much accuracy is lost when the passage has to be found. With the correct passage the reader reaches an F1 of 77.6 on answerable questions. With BM25 it reaches 71.6, with the hybrid 69.2 and with dense retrieval 63.6. In exact match the losses are 4.1, 6.1 and 10.6 points.

Almost all of that loss comes from retrieval. When the right passage was found, the reader scored the same, about 57\% EM, whichever retriever had found it. When it was not found, the score dropped to about 10\% and half of the answers were empty. Making the reader better would help little on these questions, because the answer is not in front of it. The most useful change is therefore a better first result from the retriever.

Dense retrieval was the worst retriever here, which I did not expect. BGE-M3 is reported to do much better than BM25 on Bengali retrieval benchmarks \cite{chen2024bgem3}. I did not investigate why it does not here. One possibility is that the questions of BanglaRQA were written by looking at a passage, so they share many words with it, and that favours keyword matching. Another is that passages up to 1,024 tokens are summarised into a single vector. Neither idea was tested, and no retriever was tuned. The hybrid did not beat BM25 at rank 1, but it was better at rank 10, so it could matter in a system that passes several passages to the reader.

The model is also weaker at saying that it has no answer than the published one (71.5\% against 85.4\% on unanswerable questions with the correct passage). It was trained only on correct passages, so it has never seen a passage that looks relevant but lacks the answer, and that is the situation a wrong retrieval creates. Table~\ref{tab:hitmiss} shows that in this situation it often writes nothing, which is the safe behaviour, but also that it sometimes gives an answer that is wrong.

Exact match underestimates the model. With the correct passage 56.0\% of answers match exactly, but 82.4\% have an F1 of at least 0.5, and in the examples I looked at most failures were a missing unit, an extra word or a suffix. Because of this I would not read the absolute exact-match figures as the share of questions the system really answers well. The comparison between retrievers does not depend on the choice, since the order and the significance are the same under exact match and under the relaxed match.
